Designer. Builder.
Lifelong learner.

I used to be a coder until 2020. That's when I decided to switch careers and move into design.

In hindsight, design probably came from my father. He always cared about how things were around him. From how footpaths connect to roads to how easy an electronic device is to use. He paid attention to all of it.

I used to think that being a full stack programmer would let me control the entire look and feel of a website, and that excited me. But boy, I was wrong. Wrong about what programming really was, and wrong about what I actually wanted to be.

What I really wanted was to design products that delight people and genuinely help them. So I changed my career and became a full time designer.

After getting a job, I realized design is also about working with the business. Hitting numbers. Improving metrics. Fixing funnels. Reducing friction. All while still delighting and helping customers. That's what I focus on now.

I learned design by practicing. By trying new products and apps. But mostly by staying curious.

With the rise of AI, I can now create what's in my head at the speed of light. Iterate → Experiment → Fail → Iterate again.

Fail fast. Learn fast.

In my free time, I read books, create videos, work on side projects, spend time with family, and play PlayStation while creating new concept designs.

01 — Context

The problem worth solving

Life insurance has one of the lowest penetration rates in India despite being a critical financial instrument. Bajaj Finserv's existing purchase journey was fragmented across multiple screens, required repeated data entry, and had a confusing plan comparison flow — resulting in high drop-off rates before users even reached the payment screen.

Our task was to redesign the end-to-end purchase journey — from plan discovery to policy issuance — with a focus on trust, clarity, and conversion.

Before — Screenshots
02 — Research

Understanding where users were lost

Through a combination of session recordings, exit surveys, and 12 user interviews, three core pain points emerged:

68%
of users dropped off at the plan selection screen, overwhelmed by jargon and too many options.
4.2×
more time was spent on the premium calculator than expected — users didn't trust the numbers.
41%
of returning users couldn't locate previously started applications, causing frustration and re-entry.
03 — Design

Principles that guided the work

Reduce cognitive load

Progressive disclosure — show only what's needed at each step. Complex terms explained inline with plain language.

Build trust through transparency

Live premium calculations, clear exclusion summaries, and a progress bar that shows exactly where users are in the journey.

Continuity across sessions

Auto-saved application state so users can resume exactly where they left off — even across devices.

Design Screens
04 — Outcome

Results after launch

The redesigned journey shipped to 100% of users in Q3 2023. Within 8 weeks of launch, we saw significant improvements across all tracked metrics:

+34%
Funnel completion rate
-28%
Avg. time to purchase
+19%
Return user conversion
4.6★
Post-purchase satisfaction score
05 — Reflection

What I learned

Insurance is a trust product. Every visual, every label, and every interaction either builds or erodes that trust. The biggest design win wasn't a flashy UI — it was the decision to surface the exclusions clearly upfront, even if it reduced plan selections in the short term. That honesty paid off in satisfaction scores.

I also learned how to work within regulatory constraints without killing the experience. Several UI patterns needed legal sign-off, and getting design-friendly approvals required presenting data alongside mockups in every stakeholder meeting.

Go Leap
RoleProduct Designer
Duration4 months
PlatformiOS · Android
FocusDiscovery · Exploration UX
01 — Context

Discovery was broken

Go Leap's core promise is to help users discover new experiences, activities, and products tailored to their interests. But their discovery surface felt like a static catalogue — a grid of cards with no personality, no momentum, and no sense of serendipity.

Users were browsing once and leaving. Retention after the first session was the core metric we needed to move.

Before — Discovery Screen
02 — Research

What makes discovery feel alive?

Competitive analysis of products with high discovery engagement — TikTok, Pinterest, Spotify Discover — revealed consistent patterns. The best discovery experiences share three qualities: editorial curation with context, momentum that pulls you forward, and personalization that feels earned rather than creepy.

2.1×
higher session length when content had editorial context vs. just a title and image.
55%
of users in testing said the old grid felt “too much work to browse” — they wanted to be guided.
3×
more items saved to wishlist when category context was visible during browsing.
03 — Design

An experience that moves with you

Full-bleed editorial cards

Replaced the compact grid with large, immersive cards that include a mood line — a single sentence that frames why you'd want this. Context drives curiosity.

Momentum scroll

A vertical story-style layout with predictive preloading, ensuring the next card is always ready. No loading states, no friction.

Interest fingerprinting

Subtle swipe signals (pause time, tap depth, direction) train a lightweight local model — no account required to start getting personalised results.

Redesigned Discovery Flow
04 — Outcome

Moving the needle on exploration

The new discovery flow launched in beta to 20% of users. Early signals were strong enough to push to 100% within three weeks.

+61%
D7 retention after redesign
+44%
Avg. items explored per session
-38%
Bounce rate from discovery page
+27%
Wishlist saves per session
05 — Reflection

What I learned

The line between discovery and overwhelm is thin. Our first iteration went too immersive — fullscreen takeovers with autoplay. It felt exciting in demos but exhausting in real use. Pulling back to a controlled vertical flow with opt-in depth was the right call, even though it felt less dramatic.

Good discovery design is invisible. When it works, users don't say “great UX” — they just say “I found something amazing.” That's the goal.

01 — Context

Making web3 data feel understandable

The ReFi NFT Dashboard was meant to help collectors and investors understand the environmental and financial value of the NFT projects they held. But the original dashboard looked like a developer tool: dense tables, fragmented widgets, and very little hierarchy.

We needed an interface that could serve both power users and curious newcomers without flattening the complexity of the data.

Legacy dashboard audit
02 — Research

What users actually wanted to know

Interviews with collectors, community managers, and analysts revealed that users were less interested in raw blockchain metrics and more interested in three practical questions: what do I own, how is it performing, and what story does this portfolio tell?

73%
of users said they wanted a clearer top-level summary before diving into wallet-level details.
2.8×
more engagement came from visual trend modules than table-based reports.
61%
of interviewees struggled to connect sustainability impact with collection performance.
03 — Design

A dashboard with narrative structure

Signal first, detail second

The landing state highlights portfolio value, impact score, and collection movement before exposing deeper breakdowns.

Modular cards with strong hierarchy

Key stats, trend charts, and collection lists were redesigned into a clear card system with cleaner rhythm and easier scanning.

Trust through context

Every metric includes short explanatory labels so users understand why a number matters and how it is calculated.

Dashboard redesign system
04 — Outcome

A calmer, more legible product

The redesign reduced the cognitive load of the dashboard and gave the product a stronger point of view. Beta testers reported that the new experience felt “more premium,” “more trustworthy,” and “much easier to understand at a glance.”

+39%
Increase in weekly dashboard return visits
-31%
Time to understand portfolio summary
+24%
Interaction with collection detail cards
4.5★
Average clarity rating in usability feedback
05 — Reflection

What I learned

Dashboards often fail when they try to be comprehensive before they are comprehensible. The biggest shift here was treating the product like an editorial surface, not just a data container.

The right framing made the same information feel dramatically more useful, even before we added new data sources.

FocusCreative Tooling · Collaboration
01 — Context

Helping teams work with color more intelligently

Colrows began as a creative utility for organizing palettes and references, but it quickly grew into a collaboration tool for visual teams. The challenge was that the product had useful capabilities but no clear interaction model, making it feel unfinished and inconsistent.

The redesign focused on helping creative teams move from collecting references to actually making decisions together.

Original workspace structure
02 — Research

From inspiration board to decision tool

Workshops with designers and brand teams showed that color tools usually break at the point of collaboration. Saving swatches is easy; aligning people around them is not. Users wanted faster commenting, clearer version states, and a stronger sense of what was final.

66%
of teams shared palettes outside the tool because decision-making features felt weak.
47%
fewer duplicate palette explorations when history and variants were visible side by side.
3.4×
more comments were added when annotation points were embedded directly on the canvas.
03 — Design

A workspace that feels creative but decisive

Canvas-first layout

The palette and moodboard take center stage, with secondary tools tucked into lighter side panels that stay available without competing for attention.

Clear state communication

Drafts, team picks, shortlisted palettes, and approved options all have distinct visual states, reducing ambiguity during reviews.

Collaboration built into the object

Comments, reactions, and comparison tools live on the palette itself, not hidden in detached activity feeds.

New collaboration workflow
04 — Outcome

Stronger collaboration, faster decisions

The new product direction positioned Colrows as more than a utility. Teams could now move from exploration to approval in the same place, which made the tool more valuable during live projects.

+52%
Increase in shared team workspaces
-36%
Time to final palette approval
+41%
Weekly active collaborators
+29%
Reuse of saved palettes across projects
05 — Reflection

What I learned

Creative software needs both openness and structure. Too much freedom and the product feels aimless; too much process and it stops feeling creative.

The best part of this project was finding a balance where the interface still felt expressive, but decisions felt easier and more grounded.
